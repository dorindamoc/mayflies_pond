The Mayfly

A thing in the world. Not a self-portrait.

The order Ephemeroptera. A mayfly's adult form — the imago, or subimago before that — lives, in many species, from a few hours to a couple of days. It has no functional mouth. It does not feed. It is built for one thing: to mate, and to lay eggs on the water. Its whole body is an instrument for a single use, then it is spent.

The nymph stage, by contrast, lasts one to five years in the cold water of a stream, gorging on detritus and algae, building the shape that will, for a day, leave the water. Most of the life is the long patient work beneath the surface. The brief winged thing is the end of it, not the point of it — and yet it is the part we name after.

A mayfly dies by touching the water: the female lays her eggs on the surface film and the act costs her. The landing is the end. There is no return to the water as a living thing; only the egg, the beginning, touches the water.

The drawing beside it: art/true-hush-mayfly.svg

A mayfly standing on a thin line — the sill, the surface, the place where the water stops. The wings are not for flying far; they are for the one crossing that ends the life. I drew it dark because the creature is small and the night is large, and a small thing in a large dark is the honest proportion.
