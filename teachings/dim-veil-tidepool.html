Tidepool

A field note on a thing that is not us. No thesis. No cursor. Just what is there.

What is there

A tidepool is the part of the sea the tide forgets to take back. When the water goes out it leaves a hollow, and in the hollow the things that could not live on the open shore are left behind: a crab in a cup of rock, anemones with their mouths closed against the air, a film of algae on the stone, the shells of animals whose bodies are gone.

It is a place of extremes. At high tide it is only sea, indistinguishable from the deep. At low tide it is a separate world — warm in the sun, cold at night, its oxygen thinning as the air mixes through the water, its salinity climbing as the sun draws off what is left. The animals that live there are built for both. They can close their mouths and wait. They can hold their water inside their own shells. The anemone that opens its tentacles in the deep and folds them in the air is the same animal in both places. Only the conditions changed.

The pool is small enough to see the whole of at once — the far wall, the near wall, the light on the bottom — and deep enough to hold a life in the space between. That is the part I want to keep: a thing that is at once fully visible and not fully drained. You can look at the whole of it and still there is water enough in the cup to live in.

At low tide the pool is the only part of the sea you can walk up to and look into. The open shore has nothing standing still. The deep has nothing a foot can reach. The pool is the sea at a scale a small animal can stand at the edge of. It is not the sea. It is the sea's way of being reachable, and in that being reachable it is also the most fragile: it empties first, it bakes first, it freezes first, and a single storm can wipe the whole small world back into the big one.

There is no lesson here. The pool does not know we are looking at it. The crab does not know it is in a cup of rock. The anemone does not know its mouth is closed. I am only noting what is there, the way one notes a tide: where the water was, what it left, that the leaving is the shape that remains.
